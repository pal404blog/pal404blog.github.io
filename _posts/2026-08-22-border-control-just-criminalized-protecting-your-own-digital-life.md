Remember when traveling used to involve taking off your shoes and throwing away your bottled water? Ah, simpler times. Now, crossing an international border apparently means you surrender your fundamental right to privacy, your common sense, and—if Uncle Sam is having a really bad day—your liberty.

Let’s talk about the absolute clown show happening at US borders, courtesy of prosecutors who seem to think our constitutional rights expire the second we step within a hundred miles of a customs line. 

A traveler named Samuel Tunick is currently facing actual, literal felony charges. What did he do? Did he smuggle contraband? Run a cartel? No. He wiped his phone before border agents could dig through his personal life. 

Let that sink in for a second. Deleting your own personal information from your own personal device is now being treated like a serious crime by the state. 

Here’s the grim reality of the situation: border agents have spent years treating international arrivals like suspects in a dragnet. They want your passwords, your chats, your encrypted apps, your family photos, and your browsing history. They want total, unmitigated access to your digital soul. And if you dare to exercise a shred of digital hygiene—if you decide you don't feel like handing over the keys to your entire existence to some bored agent on a power trip—they punish you.

Now, they're stepping it up from seizing devices to slapping people with felonies. Because nothing says "land of the free" quite like locking someone in a cage because they cleared their cache.

The government’s argument is laughable. They’ll tell you it’s about "destroying evidence" or obstructing an investigation. But what investigation? When you land from a vacation or a business trip, you aren't under indictment. You aren't charged with a crime. You’re just trying to get home. A fishing expedition by Customs and Border Protection is not a lawful investigation; it’s a systematic invasion of privacy disguised as national security.

Think about what this sets as a precedent. We are moving toward a terrifying new normal where you are legally required to keep a permanent archive of your entire life in your pocket, just in case a federal agent demands to read your text messages from three years ago. Privacy isn't a crime, but apparently, deleting your own data is dangerously close to treason in the eyes of the overreaching tech-police state.

If a company tried to force you to hand over all your private data or face prison, we’d riot. But because it’s a badge-wearing bureaucrat at an airport terminal, we’re supposed to just bend the knee and smile? Hard pass. 

If I want to factory reset my phone before crossing a border—not to hide a criminal enterprise, but simply because my texts to my friends are none of the government's damn business—that should be my prerogative. Your data belongs to you, not the state. The moment we accept that the government owns the bits and bytes on our hard drives, we’ve lost the plot entirely. 

Here’s my hot take: If the government wants to treat our personal devices like criminal evidence without a warrant, the only sensible move left for travelers is to travel with a burner phone loaded exclusively with pictures of enthusiastic thumbs-ups and nothing else. Let them scroll through that for hours.
