Remember when a cyber-attack meant some pimply teenager defacing a local bakery’s poorly coded WordPress site? Good times. Simpler days. 

Now, we’re living in a cyberpunk dystopia where malicious actors don't just steal your data—they actively go after your frozen peas. For the second time, supermarket freezers across Europe have been hit by a suspected cyber-attack, turning aisles of the continent's grocery stores into expensive, humming graveyards of melted ice cream and spoiled meat. 

Let that sink in for a second. We’ve spent the last decade pouring billions of dollars into generative AI art generators, crypto ponzi schemes, and metaverse real estate that nobody visits. Meanwhile, the actual physical infrastructure keeping humanity fed is running on proprietary software so fragile that a disgruntled script kiddie can apparently pull the plug on a continental cold chain from their basement. 

It’s almost impressive, if it wasn't so terrifyingly pathetic.

The tech industry loves to preach about "digital transformation" and how software is eating the world. Well, right now, software is eating our groceries, and nobody in charge seems willing to admit *why*. 

For years, industrial control systems—the hidden software brains running everything from power grids to supermarket refrigeration units—have been a complete black box. Corporations buy proprietary, closed-source firmware from vendors who care about profit margins and quarterly earnings, not security. These systems are slapped together, rushed to market, and then abandoned to rot without updates because maintaining legacy code doesn't look good on a CEO's resume. 

And then we act shocked when someone breaches the network. Shocked, I tell you!

This is where the obsessive corporate gatekeeping of technology bites us in the backside. We keep trusting multi-trillion-dollar tech giants and anonymous vendors to secure our physical lives using opaque, closed systems that nobody outside their walled gardens can audit. We treat software like a proprietary trade secret rather than public-adjacent infrastructure. 

If we leaned harder into open source principles—true transparency, community-driven auditing, and systems where code can actually be scrutinized by thousands of independent eyes instead of three overworked junior devs—maybe we wouldn't have to worry about our frozen lasagna staging a climate protest. 

When you hide how a system works behind NDA-locked doors, you aren't protecting security; you're protecting incompetence. 

The corporations running these supermarkets want us to view this as an "unprecedented sophisticated attack" by shadowy foreign hackers. That’s their favorite excuse. It shifts the blame away from their own staggering negligence. It wasn't "sophisticated." It was lazy. They bought cheap, connected IoT garbage, plugged it into the internet without basic segmentation, and crossed their fingers. 

We’ve automated our grocery stores into oblivion just so management could shave three cents off their overhead, and now we’re paying the price in liquid ice cream. 

Until we stop treating software as a magic black box and start demanding actual accountability, radical transparency, and robust engineering for the things that matter, your next trip to the supermarket is going to feel a lot less like a routine errand and a lot more like a hostage negotiation.
