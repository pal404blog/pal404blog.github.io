There is a special kind of arrogance unique to people who wear Patagonia vests to indoor meetings. It’s the unshakable belief that human history, art, and culture are nothing more than a giant backlog of unmonetized training data waiting to be vacuumed up by a language model. 

For the last few years, we’ve watched AI companies steal intellectual property from living authors, artists, and coders. We shrugged, told ourselves it was the cost of "innovation," and went back to prompting chatbots to write our emails. But the Silicon Valley machine has finally crossed a line that should make every single one of us physically ill.

They aren't just scraping the web anymore. They are literally destroying physical books. 

We’re talking about rare, centuries-old physical volumes being sent to industrial scanning facilities where spines are sliced off, pages are ripped apart, and the original artifacts are fed into automated shredders to maximize scanning speed. Once a book goes through the meat grinder of AI ingestion, it ceases to exist. It’s gone. Forever. All so some venture-backed startup can teach an algorithm how to write slightly better corporate press releases or generate mediocre fantasy art.

Let’s be completely honest about what’s happening here. This isn’t digital preservation. The Internet Archive has been digitally scanning books for decades with actual respect for the source material, trying to make knowledge accessible without turning world heritage into confetti. What the modern AI gold rush is doing is the exact opposite. It is a scorched-earth extraction policy. It’s the digital equivalent of burning down the Library of Alexandria because you need charcoal to draw a smiley face.

The irony is thick enough to choke on. The tech industry has spent the last thirty years promising us a paperless, friction-free utopian future where physical objects matter less and less. Yet, when their insatiable algorithms run out of clean internet data to steal, they suddenly remember that the real world holds centuries of accumulated human thought. And how do they interact with that physical world? By destroying it. They treat the tangible history of human civilization like disposable packaging.

We’re told this is progress. We’re told that if we don’t let billionaires pulverize our literary heritage, China will win the AI race, or we’ll miss out on the next iteration of a chatbot that tells us how many rocks to eat. It’s a pathetic, small-minded justification for cultural vandalism. 

We are letting a bunch of tech monopolists act as judge, jury, and executioner for the physical manifestations of human thought. If a rogue library set fire to rare books to heat their offices, they’d be raided by the FBI. But when a corporation does it to pad their valuation, our politicians hand them subsidies and invite them to keynote tech summits. 

If we don't demand an emergency push to properly scan and protect rare books before these corporate vultures get to them, we are going to wake up in a world where the only record of our past is whatever a localized server farm decided was profitable enough to remember. 

Hot take: If your trillion-dollar AI model requires the literal, irreversible destruction of physical human history to function, your technology isn't revolutionary—it's a parasite, and we should pull the plug before there's nothing left of us to train on.
