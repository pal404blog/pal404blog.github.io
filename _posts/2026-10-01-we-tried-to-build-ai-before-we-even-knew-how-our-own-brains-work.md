Let’s be honest for a second. The entire artificial intelligence boom is built on a massive, wildly arrogant assumption: that we actually understand how the human brain works. 

Spoiler alert: We don’t. Not even close.

For decades, neuroscientists have treated the brain like a giant switchboard. Neurons fire, messages pass through synapses, and—boom—you remember where you parked your car or figure out how to write a Python script. It's a clean, reductionist view that tech bros and silicon valley evangelics fell in love with because it sounds an awful lot like a microprocessor. *Input, process, output.* Easy. 

Except, biology doesn't care about our neat little engineering analogies. 

Recent research has blown that outdated switchboard model completely out of the water. Scientists are mapping the brain and finding something far more chaotic and mesmerizing: complex, overlapping waves of electrical and chemical activity rippling across neural tissue. We’re not digital computers crunching 1s and 0s. We’re walking, talking ocean currents of fluctuating energy. 

And it turns out these waves aren't just background noise. They’re doing the heavy lifting. They coordinate memory, regulate consciousness, and process sensory input in ways that make our current neural networks look like a toddler playing with an abacus. 

So, what does Big Tech do when faced with this humbling reality? Do we pause, go back to the drawing board, and admit we’ve barely scratched the surface of intelligence? 

Of course not. We double down. 

We take our primitive, brute-force transformer models—which burn enough electricity to power a small European nation just to tell you a joke about a penguin—and we slap the word "cognitive" on the marketing brochure. We pretend we've cracked the code of sentience because an LLM can pass a bar exam, completely ignoring the fact that our own skulls contain a fluid, wave-driven orchestra of biology that we can barely measure, let alone replicate.

It’s hilarious, really. We’re desperately trying to build synthetic minds while treating our own biological ones like obsolete hardware. 

The irony is thick enough to cut with a knife. For years, AI researchers have chased AGI by adding more layers, more parameters, and more compute. They’ve treated the brain as a solved problem just waiting for better GPUs. But these new wave discoveries suggest that intelligence isn't just about massive scale or clever algorithms. It's about rhythm, harmony, and a kind of fluid adaptability that silicon simply doesn't have yet—and frankly, that our current mechanistic paradigm is completely blind to.

Until we start building computing architectures that respect the messy, wave-based reality of actual neurobiology, our "advanced" AI is nothing more than a very fast, very expensive autocomplete. We are trying to write symphonies with a hammer, wondering why we only ever hear crashing noises.

Here is my hot take: Until Silicon Valley humbles itself, admits it's operating in the dark, and actually learns from how biological brains surf these complex waves instead of just stealing their output, "Artificial General Intelligence" will remain what it has always been: a marketing buzzword for investors who skipped science class.
