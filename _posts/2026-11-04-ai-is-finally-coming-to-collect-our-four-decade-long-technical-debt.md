Remember when "move fast and break things" was the gospel according to Silicon Valley? Turns out, we broke things so badly that the bill is finally due. And the collector isn't some angry bank; it’s artificial intelligence.

For the last forty years, the tech industry has been playing an epic game of digital Jenga. We built skyscrapers of software on top of rotting foundations, duct-taped together with legacy code, rushed patches, and a collective prayer that nobody would look too closely at the architecture. Corporations loved it because it meant shipping features yesterday. Developers tolerated it because, well, rent is expensive. 

We called it technical debt, but let’s be honest with each other: it was sheer, unadulterated laziness masquerading as agility. 

Now, AI has entered the chat, and it’s acting like a ruthless auditor with zero patience for our past mistakes. 

Take the recent nightmares coming out of cybersecurity research, where AI is being weaponized to sniff out vulnerabilities in ancient, half-forgotten codebases—like decades-old PHP implementations—turning minor oversight into remote-code-execution disasters with terrifying speed. AI doesn't need to grab a cup of coffee or pull an all-nighter to read through millions of lines of garbage code you abandoned in 2011. It just looks at your sloppy architecture, smiles its algorithmic smile, and exploits it.

We spent decades pretending that security through obscurity would save us. "Who's going to audit our bespoke, undocumented mess from the nineties?" we asked ourselves while pushing to production on a Friday afternoon. 

Well, algorithms are. That's who.

The great irony of the current AI boom is that everyone is obsessed with what AI can *create*. We’re marveling at its ability to paint, write mediocre pop songs, and generate boilerplate React components. But the real story isn't creation; it's exposure. AI is holding up an unforgiving mirror to the digital house of cards we’ve built since the dawn of the commercial internet. 

Every shortcut your CTO took to hit a quarterly goal? An AI can spot it in milliseconds. Every deprecated library you swore you'd update "next sprint"? An attack vector waiting to be automated. 

For years, human hackers had to be smart, patient, and lucky. They had to dig through mountains of spaghetti code to find the one weak thread. Now, we’ve handed bad actors—and automated defense systems alike—a tireless machine that treats our collective technical debt like an open buffet. 

And honestly? Good. 

We deserved this reckoning. For too long, the software industry has operated with a level of negligence that would get civil engineers thrown in prison. If a bridge builder used cheap, rotting timber and told the public, "Don't worry, we'll patch it with duct tape later," they'd lose their license. But in tech? We gave them venture capital.

Fixing this isn't going to be cheap, and it certainly isn't going to be pretty. You can't prompt-engineer your way out of forty years of structural rot. Companies are going to have to do the unglamorous, expensive work of actually rewriting their core systems from the ground up. They're going to have to pay developers to build things right, not just fast.

So go ahead and panic about AI taking your job, or stealing your art, or whatever corporate dystopia keeps you up at night. But remember the real threat: AI is simply weaponizing our own sloppiness. If your tech stack is built on a foundation of quick fixes and crossed fingers, you don't need to worry about the robot apocalypse. Your own past is coming for you first.

**Hot take:** If your company's entire cybersecurity strategy relies on the fact that "nobody understands how this legacy system works," you're not secure—you're just waiting to be made an example of by a script kiddie using an LLM.
